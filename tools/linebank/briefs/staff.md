# This family: your staff

Your assistant coaches talk to you, coach to coach: before your game day talk, in the staff meeting the morning after a game, and when you interview a coach for a job on your staff. The speaker is a coach (a coordinator or position coach on your staff, or a candidate), at your level.

- `gameday.staff.*` is a coordinator's quiet read of the locker room before you speak: good (the room is right), amped (too hot), tight (nervous), flat (no life), hollow (sounds ready but isn't), captain (a captain, sometimes named `{player}`, could say it better than you today). One or two sentences, said low, as advice, never an order. It must read before any game, so no opponent, score or result.
- `staffmeet.case.*` is a coordinator making his case for a change after a game: more pressure, more zone coverage, running it more, throwing it more. Real football reasons: protection, the boundary corner, light boxes, third-down rates, the quarterback's eyes, the front's rotation. Name no player, opponent or stat. Two or three sentences, coach talk.
- `staffmeet.reply.*` is his answer when you agreed (yes), met him halfway (half) or overruled him (no). Professional, in his own voice; a hot or proud coach may show it, a loyal one takes it.
- `staff.interview.open` is a candidate opening his interview for a job on your staff: `{role}` is the job in lower case ("offensive line coach"), `{team}` your team's name, `{came_up}` where he came up ("at Toledo"). Who he is, how he coaches, why this job. He speaks to the head coach who may hire him.

## Voice

Coaches talk in football shorthand (fronts, pressures, the boundary, the field side, pass pro, tempo, the call sheet) but stay clear to someone who knows the game. High school assistants are often teachers who coach; college staffs recruit and work long hours; NFL staffs live on film and the call sheet. A coach's tags (hot, calm, climber, loyal, old, young, expro, strict, warm, cold, she) are how he sounds. No family, no spouses, no days of the week.
