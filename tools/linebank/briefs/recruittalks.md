# This family: a recruit's side in the recruiting talks

You are the head coach of a college program. You recruit high school players and players who enter the transfer portal, by phone, in a family's living room (a home visit) and in a sit-down on your campus. Every line you write is said TO you by the recruit's side. You are the one being sold on, questioned and judged: they decide whether you earn the player.

## The two sides who speak

- FAMILY SIDE, a high school recruit (a junior or senior): the speaker is whoever decides in his family, tagged `parent` (a parent or guardian: "my son", "my boy"), `grandparent` ("my grandson"), `handler` (a trainer or 7-on-7 coach who steers him: business, "the kid", "my player"), or `himself` (the 17-year-old speaking for himself, first person, plain, short, a little guarded). A parent may also be `loud` (loud at games), `online` (posts about his football) or `absent` (rarely around). `{name}` is the recruit's first name; only a `parent`, `grandparent` or `handler` says it, never `himself`.
- TRANSFER SIDE, a player in the portal: a college player (tag `college`), 18 to 23, who decides for himself and has already played. He talks like a college player: past snaps, an old coach, a degree, a deal. He has position, personality and stage tags (`transfer`, `redshirt`, `senior`...). Never name his old school or coach.

A line tagged for one side is never said by the other, so never mix the sides' tags on a line. An untagged line is said by both: its words must fit a parent in a living room and a transfer on the phone alike (so no "my son", no "{name}", no class year, no old coach). Untagged lines carry the part (a speaker hears the part again and again, and a tagged line fits only some speakers), so most lines are untagged. Mix, for a part both sides speak: about 55% untagged, a fifth family side (spread over `parent`, `grandparent`, `handler`, `himself`, `loud`, `online`, `absent`), a fifth `college` lines (with a personality or stage tag where the words need it; a position tag on at most one line in fifteen), the rest a light regional or era touch on either side.

## Levels work differently here (this overrides the general rules)

A recruit's side talks across high school and college at once. Scholarship, NIL, the collective, campus, offers, official visits, signing day, the portal, the draft and the league are plain words for them: write them freely and do NOT add a level tag for them. In this family `college` is not a word marker, it means the TRANSFER side as a speaker (a college player in the portal). Never use `hs` or `nfl` tags. Never use NFL-only words (the cap, a contract, free agency, the practice squad).

## The setting

- Where the talk happens is never stated: a call, a living room and a campus sit-down all use the same line. No "on the phone", "in your office", "this couch", "your campus tour".
- Real life: families are careful with money talk, protective, proud, sometimes overwhelmed by the attention; some have done this before with an older child (never say so unless the part says so). Handlers talk business. A recruit speaking for himself is plain and a little guarded. A transfer is blunter: he has been through it.
- A tag is a fact about the speaker, not a topic: never write the tag's word into the line ("I'm a competitor", "I'm loose"), and a `redshirt`, `walkon` or `senior` tag doesn't make a story ("I earned my spot") the line can lean on. Write what the person would say and let the tag be the reason he can.
- He has not visited, been shown around or seen anything of your school unless the part says so: no "the drive", "the tour", "that building", "showing me around".
- The part's key phrase is the idea, not a refrain: if the part is about money being tight, "money's tight" may open at most one line in fifteen. Find the concrete way a real person says it (the bills, a second job, what a visit costs, no cushion, a number that has to be real). Two lines of a part must never lean on the same phrase.
- ONE KEY, ONE CHECKED FACT. The game checks the one fact a part is named for before it says the part's lines (his family has money trouble, his brother plays college ball, you are new). A line asserts that fact and nothing else stored about him: never how many parents he has or who raised him (no "my parents", no "my mom and dad", no "both of us"), siblings, jobs, a move, a death, his grades, another school's offer, a visit, your record, unless the part's own fact is that. Say "my family", "my people", "back home".
- In an untagged line never "we", "us" or "our": a transfer decides for himself and says "I". Put "we" on a `parent`, `grandparent` or `handler` line.
- Never write a coach's name; you are filled in as {coach} ("Coach" and your last name).
- They test you. They ask real questions, push on weak answers, say what they have heard from other schools (never naming one), and reward a straight answer. They are never rude, never swear, never talk down.
- Never a real school, coach or player's name; never a ranking, a star count, an offer sheet or a number of any kind. Never an assertion the world may contradict: no record, no score, no stat, no day or date, no named person.
- The part's meaning is a fact about the recruit or the talk. Write what a real person in that spot would actually say, in their own voice, plainly. Specific beats generic: a family worried about money talks about the bills, the plane tickets, a week off work, not "financial concerns".

## What the parts are

- `portal.*`: the transfer's side of a portal call. `portal.ask.<drive>` is his first question about what he weighs most; `portal.tell` answers "tell me what you're looking for" with `{first}` then `{second}`; `portal.react.<good|flat|bad>` is his reaction to your pitch; `portal.promise.<knows|believes|doubts>` his reaction to your promise of a starting job or a real role.
- `recruit.open.<warm|polite|guarded>` and `recruit.close.<warm|flat|cool>` begin and end a talk, by how he feels about you. They are heard in every talk, so they carry the most variety. An opening never asks what he wants; a close never names a decision date.
- `recruit.record.*`: he asks about your track record at his position (`{pos}`), then reacts to your answer. `{pos}` reads QB, RB, WR, TE, OT, IOL, EDGE, DT, LB, CB, S, K, P or LS: write "at {pos}", "the {pos} room", never "{pos}s", never "a {pos}".
- `recruit.aspect.<tradition|facilities|atmosphere|brand|conference|campus|prestige>.<good|cool>`: you pitched one of your strengths; he reacts, impressed or cool.
- `recruit.bg.*`: his background raised in his own way, one fact per part (money, how he was raised, a job lost, a move, a death, a new baby, a mentor, NIL money, a brother in college, football in the family, grades, ambition). A fact of their life, personal and plain, never a speech, never naming who. The part says whom it may name.
- `recruit.want.*`, `recruit.history.*`, `recruit.other.*`, `recruit.committed.*`, `recruit.you.*`, `recruit.team.*`, `recruit.visit.*`: what he wants, what has happened between you, who else is in the picture.
- `transfer.why.*` and `transfer.role.*`: a transfer explains why he left and what his role was.

## Voice and shape

Lines are one or two short sentences. Questions, statements, fragments and wry asides all belong; no speeches. Contractions and plain words. Never "I appreciate you taking the time" in a dozen forms: find the real thing a person says. The owner hates stale text: two lines of a part must never feel like the same line.
