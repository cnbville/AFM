## 1. College decides more, plus a first-year adjustment (your answer: A, made dynamic)

**What the sim does today** (two test classes of 250,000):
- Five-stars arrive at college rated about 65–66 overall. The typical upperclassman starting at FBS is about 59, so five-stars come in ahead of the veterans, and 91% of them start as true freshmen.
- Four-stars arrive at about 58, level with those veterans, and 41% start.
- Growth happens too early. Every star level gains only about 7 points over four college years, against 14–25 points from 14 to 17.
- A player plays at his full rating from his first day at a new level, and his role comes straight from that rating.

### Rawer arrivals (the original 1A)
Move most growth from high school into college, especially strength and technique. Then high school dominance says more about early maturity, and college decides more.

### First-year adjustment at every step up
This covers high school to college, college to the pros, and a transfer up to a higher level. In his first year at the new level, a player plays below his rating and his coaches trust him less. The gap closes through the year, so it delays players rather than capping them.

How far below his rating he plays is not fixed. It depends on:
1. **How big the step is.** High school to college and FCS or D2 to the pros are the biggest steps. FBS to the pros is smaller.
2. **His position.** Linemen, quarterbacks and coverage defenders adjust slowest. Running backs, receivers and pass rushers, the pure athletes, adjust fastest.
3. **Polish or rawness.** Refined technique (route running, hand use, footwork) carries over at once, as with Smith-Njigba and Nabers. A raw athlete like Manu needs time.
4. **Football IQ and make-up.** Awareness, play recognition, poise, coachability and work ethic.
5. **Physical readiness.** His strength and size against the new level, such as an 18-year-old lineman facing 22-year-olds.
6. **Fit and luck.** Scheme fit and coaching, plus a random spread. Some first-rounders struggle, and some UDFAs thrive from day one. Often those UDFAs were simply better than the scouts thought.

**Every trial card will say why.** For example:
- "Adjusted fast: polished routes (88) and poise (15), and receivers adjust quickly."
- "Slow start: FCS to the pros, raw technique (61), and linemen adjust slowly."

## 2. Leaving early (your answer: A, plus your new layer)

**Your question:** could this be part of the missing volatility? Yes, partly. Today 97% of first-rounders and 75% of all picks leave the first year they can. So nobody has a return year that could raise his stock or sink it.

**Proposal to discuss. I won't build it until we agree.** Each player who is eligible to leave weighs:
- where teams project him;
- how his last season went (after a bad year, returning to rebuild his stock is more likely);
- what staying is worth in NIL money, which is most for quarterbacks and stars at big programs;
- which teams would likely pick him (some players avoid a bad franchise);
- his age, eligibility left and injury history;
- his personality.

Returning is a real gamble. Another season brings form, injuries and development, so his stock can rise or fall.

**Landing spot matters after the draft.** The worst teams pick first. A bad organisation (weak coaching, a weak supporting cast, rushing him onto the field) can stall a top pick, as with Darnold and the Jets, and a better situation later can revive him. Today pro coaching is a random roll that has nothing to do with the team that drafted him.

**Questions for you:**
- How strong should the NIL pull be?
- Should a player know roughly which team would draft him? Real players get a good idea from projections and pre-draft visits.

## 3. Pro careers (your answer: A, plus a free-agent market and portal tracking)

- Released players go into a free-agent pool that is tracked from season to season.
- Teams sign from the pool by need, rating, age and cost. Players can re-sign with their own team. Unsigned players stay in the pool and can retire from it.
- The college portal works the same way: players enter, get offers or don't, and some end up without a school.
- Cards show every move: released, signed, re-signed, entered the portal, landed.

## 6. Position value (your answer: A, plus situational weights)

Each team weighs a position by two things:
- how much the position is worth in general: quarterbacks, edge rushers and tackles up, running backs down;
- its own roster.

So a team with an elite defense missing only a corner will reach for a generational corner. A team with a young franchise quarterback won't take another one early.

## What calls 3, 5, 6 and 7 and your ideas need: real teams

Free agency, team needs, landing spots, depth charts and the portal all need actual teams with rosters. Today the sim runs one class of players on its own. Their teammates and opponents don't exist. That's why roles come from rating cutoffs and why no team can "need" a corner.

So trial 5 would become the first league sim:
- 32 NFL teams and the college programs, each with a full roster;
- every class simulated together, season by season.

It is the biggest build so far, so it's your call (decision card in the thread).
