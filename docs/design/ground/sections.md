## Section 1: The map, your ground as a place (brainstorm, 2026-11-05)

What it's for: the map should feel like real country you're getting to know, not a chart. Grey where you're a stranger, alive where you've worked, with names, distances and history that make it a place.

Real facts behind it:
- Coaches and recruiting sites name areas by nickname and area code: "the 757" (Hampton Roads, Virginia), "the 305" (Miami), "the DMV" (DC, Maryland and Virginia), "the Valley" (Texas's Rio Grande Valley).
- Areas really differ: south Florida is crowded with speed and with every program's recruiters; small-town east Texas and south Georgia are worked school by school; west Texas is hours of driving between towns.
- Rosters, box scores and stars are public everywhere. What isn't public is which kids are real prospects and what their coaches really think. That's what ground gives you.

Built on: the world's recruiting areas (`HighSchool.area`: state and county), real towns per county, state football culture (`StateInfo.culture`), school size, money and resources, town climate (`data/real-world/town_climate.csv`), the NFL scouting regions (`world/pro/scouts.rs`, `covers`), and the world's 30 years of players.

Brainstorm (each item yes or no):
1. **Names the way coaches talk.** Areas carry their real nicknames and area codes ("the 757", "the Valley", "the DMV"), and your staff use them.
2. **Fog you clear.** The public view shows everywhere (rosters, stars, box scores). What your ground knows (which kids are prospects, the coach's word) stays fogged in an area until your standing opens it, so the map fills in as you grind.
3. **Each area has a character, in words.** You learn it as you work there. Examples: "speed everywhere, and every school is recruited by everyone"; "small towns, big linemen, the coach decides". It comes from the world's own data: state football culture, school sizes, money.
4. **Travel is real.** Drive and flight times come from the towns' real positions (new data). This week's routes for your men are drawn on the map, and each area shows how many schools a day it allows.
5. **The map moves with the year and the day.** The spring sweep leaves trails, June camps glow, and on Friday nights the map lights up: your men at games, scores coming in, a breakout pinging.
6. **History on the map.** Pins show where your signees and finds came from and the area's own legends from the world's 30 years. Rivals' pipelines are drawn as lines to their campuses.
7. **Your roots.** Your hometown and your old jobs are ground you already hold on day one, and a kid from your hometown knows your name.
8. **Owners' colors.** Each area is shaded in its leader's colors, with contested areas marked and your standing beside each.
9. **The high school map is your building.** Your building is drawn as a floor plan (weight room, gym, halls, field house), with your district around it and the colleges that recruit you as an outer ring.
10. **The NFL map is your department.** Your scouts are pins across their regions, every college down to the NAIA is a dot with your standing there, and your coaching tree is marked.
11. **Every school is a place.** Its page shows its colors, stadium and coach, how many kids it sends to whom, and how long its tape goes back.

Pick: yes to all 11; the heart is 2 (fog you clear), 5 (the map moves with the year) and 9 (your building).

## Section 2: Standing, the grind and what it unlocks (brainstorm, 2026-11-06)

What it's for: standing is what you grind for. Every visit, call, honest word and kid you help place moves it, and it decides what people tell you, how true it is, how early it comes and who hears first. It should feel earned person by person, and you should feel it slipping before it goes.

Cain's rule it starts from (GR16): every coach answers, because placing kids is a chain reaction that lifts his school. Standing changes what's in the answer, not whether there is one.

Real facts behind it:
- The head coach is the first call. College staffs call a kid's head coach before anyone else. A small-school coach works to get his kids seen (tape, calls, camps); a powerhouse coach fields dozens of staffs every spring (commonly said).
- Ties are kept in person. College staffs speak at high school clinics. Texas's THSCA Convention and Coaching School each July draws over 16,000 coaches, administrators and exhibitors, with college head coaches such as Steve Sarkisian and Sonny Dykes leading sessions ([thsca.com](https://thsca.com/convention); [Lone Star Gridiron, 2024](https://lonestargridiron.com/2024/07/92nd-annual-thsca-convention-and-coaching-school-preview-with-brian-polk-coo-of-thsca/)).
- Schools that place players draw players: IMG Academy, Mater Dei and St. Thomas Aquinas draw kids from far outside their towns, and each state's rules decide how freely a kid can transfer.
- Recruiters pass names along: a kid one staff can't take often goes to a friend at a smaller program, and high school coaches remember who helped (commonly said).
- An NFL scout's best sources at a college are often the trainer and strength coach, not the head coach (commonly said). The school's staff decides which scouts get its tape, its medical word and its honest word.

Today in the code: `folk/network.rs` keeps your contacts, each with a warmth from 0 to 1 that visits and calls move (`reach_out`, `touch`); `folk/tips.rs` decides how straight a source is. A high school's `prestige` is fixed when the world is made (only the legacy bond adds 0.03, `legacy/hs.rs`), and it sets how much recruiting exposure its kids get. Kids change high schools only when you, as a high school coach, pitch them (`scouting/pitch.rs`, where the prestige gap between the two schools sways the family).

Brainstorm (each item yes or no; everything proposed, not confirmed):
1. **The ladder.** Four levels with each person: Stranger, Known, Trusted, Home. At college it's with high school coaches, trainers, counselors and families; at high school, with each college staff; at the NFL, with each college's coaches, trainer and strength coach, through your scouts.
2. **A why on every standing.** Each one shows its reasons: "Trusted: four visits this year, his Reyes started for you, you helped place Simmons at Elon."
3. **What standing changes (GR16's open half).** Everyone answers; standing sets how much, how true, how early and who hears first. Stranger: the public answer a day or two later ("6-2, good grades, good kid"). Known: his real list of prospects, the same day. Trusted: the truth about a kid (his work, his family, the injury he plays through) and tape before you ask. Home: he calls you first, before the kid is public.
4. **Busy coaches answer in order.** A powerhouse coach with ten prospects and dozens of staffs calling answers in order of standing, then program level, so a stranger hears last and gets the short version. A small-school coach answers everyone fast and pushes his kids on you, because he needs them seen.
5. **The chain reaction (Cain's idea).** A high school whose kids do well in college and the NFL climbs: its prestige rises, more staffs visit, its kids get more exposure, and better kids come to it (move-ins, and transfers where the state's rules allow). A school whose kids stop producing slowly falls back. New in the world: today prestige is fixed and kids move only on your pitch.
6. **Placing kids builds standing.** The strongest way to win a coach is to help his kids, even ones you don't take: a call to a friend's staff gets his kid an offer at an FCS or Division II school. At the high school seat, a college trusts you more each time a kid you vouched for pans out. At the NFL, a college staff warms to you when you give its overlooked senior a tryout.
7. **What builds it.** Being there (visits, calls, his clinic, your camp), your word coming true, help, how his kids fared with you (played, graduated, or buried and transferred), and your program's level and years. Each is weighed by your man's skill with people and the coach's own nature (loyal, wary, out for himself).
8. **How it fades.** Slowly with time away and a rival's visits. Sharply with a broken promise: an offer pulled, or a kid buried after you told his coach he'd play. When your man leaves, his own ties go with him.
9. **Warnings before it slips.** Before a level drops you hear it at the hour it's noticed (a road call, the 5 AM map), with the reason and the fix: "Columbus is slipping: no visit since March, Georgia was there twice."
10. **Moving up is a moment.** Each new level comes in that person's own words, at his hour ("From now on you hear about my guys first"), and the morning map shades the area.
11. **Standing lives with people.** Each coach, trainer and counselor holds his own. An area's level is its people together, weighted by how much talent each one controls.
12. **How long it takes (numbers proposed).** Known in a few visits, Trusted in a year or two of keeping your word, Home in three to five years, usually after a kid of his did well with you.
13. **Yours and the program's.** Your own ties go with you when you change jobs. The program's ties stay with it, and your successor starts with them.

Pick: yes to all 13; the heart is 3 (what standing changes), 5 (the chain reaction) and 6 (placing kids).
